\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

My baseline (Codabench 954621), a beta--binomial update with prior strength 4, predicts $(4p_0 + k)/(4 + n)$ for a pair with $k$ successes in $n$ acquired labels, where $p_0$ is a prior success rate from the subject's metadata (Appendix~\ref{app:model}). V1 to V5 build on one another; V5 is the final entry, and Table~\ref{tab:formative} gives the Codabench submission of each version.

Throughout, $\theta_s$ is subject $s$'s ability on the benchmark being predicted. Labels on other benchmarks are not used, and item difficulties are centered within each benchmark, so the benchmark's mean difficulty is absorbed into $\theta_s$. I write $\sigma$ for the logistic function and $\kappa(v) = \sqrt{1 + \pi v/8}$ for the factor of the probit approximation $\mathbb{E}[\sigma(x)] \approx \sigma(\mu/\kappa(v))$ for $x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, v)$ \citep{mackay1992evidence}.

\subsection{Ability prior and grid posterior (V1, V3)}
V1 gives every item of a pair the success probability $\sigma(\theta_s)$, with a normal prior $\theta_s \sim \mathcal{N}(m_s, s_\theta^2)$, $s_\theta = 1.5$, so the success rate is logit-normal. The prior mean is $m_s = \lambda\,(g(s) - \bar g)$, clipped to $[-6, 6]$, where $g(s)$ is the subject part of the baseline's metadata regression (release date, a ``thinking'' variant flag and the provider, plus a shrunken residual for subjects seen in training; Appendix~\ref{app:model}) and $\lambda$ is a scale. After $k$ successes in $n$ own labels,
\[
  p(\theta \mid \text{labels}) \propto \mathcal{N}(\theta; m_s, s_\theta^2)\, \sigma(\theta)^k\, \sigma(-\theta)^{n-k},
\]
which I evaluate on a fixed grid and summarize by the posterior mean of $\sigma(\theta)$, an expected-a-posteriori estimate \citep{bock1982eap}. To limit the loss if metadata do not carry over, V1 used $\lambda = 0.5$, although $\lambda = 1$ was better offline by 0.0016 (SE 0.0004; scenario analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:model}). For V3 I set $\lambda = 0$; as no labels are passed at budget 0, every prediction there is exactly 0.5. This is the one model setting I chose directly from formative feedback, and offline it was worse (Table~\ref{tab:offline}).

\subsection{Item pooling (V2)}
In V2 items differ in difficulty. A Rasch model \citep{rasch1960} with a normal prior on difficulty gives
\begin{equation}
  P(y_{si} = 1) = \sigma(\kappa_0\theta_s - z_i), \qquad z_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2), \qquad \kappa_0 = \kappa(\tau^2),
  \label{eq:rasch}
\end{equation}
with $\tau = 2$ (not tuned) and $\kappa_0 \approx 1.60$; a larger $z_i$ is a harder item. With $\phi_s = \kappa_0\theta_s$ this is the organizers' $\sigma(\phi_s - z_i)$ \citep{truong2025reeval}. The factor $\kappa_0$ keeps $\theta$ on V1's scale: by the probit approximation, the average over an unknown difficulty is $\sigma(\theta_s)$ to within 0.0101 for $\theta_s \in [-6, 6]$.

A difficulty can only be learned from other subjects' labels on the same item, which the predictor sees when a draw puts several subjects on one benchmark. Few pairs in my formative draws shared a benchmark, so pooling seldom had partner labels. I estimate difficulties with a two-stage calibration \citep{ban2001online,truongkoyejo2026aims}: each partner label becomes an observation of the item's difficulty, given that partner's ability estimated from its \emph{other} labels, and the observations are combined with the prior on $z_j$ into a posterior mean $\hat z_j$ and variance $\omega_j^2$, which then enter the target subject's likelihood and predictions (Appendix~\ref{app:model}). Items carry no identifier, so I match them across subjects by content and features. With no partner labels, V2 gives exactly V1's predictions.

\subsection{LLM difficulty ratings and the gate (V4)}
For V4 I also had Claude Opus 5.5 \citep{anthropic2026opus55} rate how hard each item is. The rater sees the item's content and features with a fixed rubric (Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}), is told not to solve the item, and returns one integer from 1 (almost every AI system released in 2023--2026 completes it) through 5 (about half do) to 10 (almost none do); a refusal or a value outside 1--10 counts as unrated. Offline I had it rate 1,049 public items, one per problem, which through shared content cover the 1,379 rated items of Table~\ref{tab:data}. Against Rasch difficulties fitted separately on each benchmark to all subjects' public responses (Appendix~\ref{app:model}), the Spearman correlations were 0.82 (366 rated problems), 0.28 (400 items), 0.44 (233) and 0.77 (50) on MathArena, Multi-SWE-bench, REAL and ResearchCodeBench. These benchmarks are public, and the rater may partly remember how hard their items are.

A rated item $j$ with rating $r_j$ gets the prior
\begin{equation}
  z_j \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(\beta_t\,(r_j - \bar r),\ \rho_t^2\bigr),
  \nonumber
\end{equation}
which replaces the prior $\mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rasch} in the second stage and is used directly when the item has no partner labels; this is an explanatory item model in the sense of \citet{deboeck2004explanatory}. Here $\bar r$ is the mean rating of the rated items already labeled on the benchmark, by the target subject or by partners. Ratings thus enter only through their difference from that mean, never through their absolute level; until a rated item is labeled, and so always at budget 0, they have no effect. The slope $\beta$ and residual standard deviation $\rho$ come from a regression of the Rasch difficulties on the ratings, pooled within benchmarks: $\beta = 0.788$ and $\rho = 1.904$ for the submission, which uses all four rated benchmarks; the offline evaluation refits them without the held-out benchmark. A trust level $t \in [0, 1]$ interpolates between V3's prior and the fitted regression,
\begin{equation}
  \beta_t = t\beta, \qquad \rho_t^2 = \tau^2 - t^2 \max(\tau^2 - \rho^2, 0).
  \label{eq:trust}
\end{equation}
Because $\rho < \tau$ in every fit, $t = 0$ gives V3 and $t = 1$ gives $(\beta, \rho)$; because $\rho$ is close to $\tau$, $t$ acts almost only through the slope. I chose $t = 0.5$ ($\beta_t = 0.394$, $\rho_t = 1.976$) with a rule fixed before the run: it had the smallest worst-case regret over real, half-noisy (Appendix~\ref{app:rules}) and shuffled ratings (Section~\ref{sec:results}). A trust level's regret in a scenario is its Brier ALC minus that of the best trust level in the same scenario. With these values, after one success on an item rated 3, an item rated 8 gets 0.417 under V4 and 0.647 under V3.

Ratings that point the wrong way hurt. Offline, V4 without the gate was worse than V3 by 0.027 Brier ALC with the ratings reversed and by 0.0065 with them shuffled, and with the gate by 0.011 and 0.0048. The gate makes V4 predict as V3 whenever the benchmark has at least three labels on rated items, both outcomes occur among them, and the failed items have the lower mean rating. During scoring V4 requests ratings of the hidden items with the same prompt (Section~\ref{sec:llm}).

\subsection{Guessed subject priors (V5)}
Ratings say nothing at budget 0, and on all three hosted draws scored with a metadata prior, the budget-0 predictions did worse than a constant 0.5 (Section~\ref{sec:results}). V5 instead asks Claude how strong the subject is. The request shows the subject's recorded fields, exactly as the platform passes them, and one item of the benchmark as an example, and asks what percentage of the benchmark's items the subject completes (Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}); it names no benchmark and contains no labels. During scoring the example is whichever item of the pair is predicted first. Before building V5 I checked, with a rule fixed in advance, whether Claude knows subject strength at all. With three example items per benchmark, the Pearson correlation between the logit of its guess and Rasch ability, computed within each benchmark and averaged over the four, was 0.818 (0.957 on MathArena; Appendix~\ref{app:model}), against 0.611 for V1's metadata score; the single-example guesses that V5 uses reach 0.771.

The guess $q_s$ enters through a calibration. On the public pairs, an ordinary least-squares fit of the logit of each pair's success rate on $\operatorname{logit} q_s$, both clipped to $[0.01, 0.99]$, gives an intercept $a_g$, a slope $c_g$ and a residual standard deviation $s_g$. A subject with a guess then gets
\begin{equation}
  \theta_s \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(t_g\,(a_g + c_g \operatorname{logit} q_s),\ (1 - t_g^2)\,s_\theta^2 + t_g^2 s_g^2\bigr),
  \label{eq:guessprior}
\end{equation}
with $q_s$ clipped to $[0.01, 0.99]$ and the mean to $[-6, 6]$ as in V1. This replaces V4's $\mathcal{N}(0, s_\theta^2)$ for the target subject and for every partner whose labels are pooled. The guess trust $t_g$ interpolates as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:trust}: $t_g = 0$ is V4 and $t_g = 1$ trusts the calibration fully. A subject without a guess keeps V4's prior. I chose $t_g = 0.75$ by the same minimax-regret rule as the rating trust, over real guesses, shuffled guesses and every guess moved by $\pm 1$ on the logit scale (Section~\ref{sec:results}). With the submission's calibration (Appendix~\ref{app:model}), every item of a pair gets the prior mean of $\sigma(\theta_s)$ at budget 0: a guess of 10\% gives 0.25, 30\% gives 0.40 and 90\% gives 0.76, closer to 0.5 than the guesses themselves.
